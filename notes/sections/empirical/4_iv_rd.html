Instrumental Variables and Regression Discontinuity

Instrumental variables

The problem: OLS uses the wrong variation

Suppose the causal equation is

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i, \]

where \(\beta_1\) is the causal effect of \(X\) on \(Y\). OLS can recover \(\beta_1\) only when the variation in \(X\) is unrelated to the unobserved determinants collected in \(u\):

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X_i,u_i)=0. \]

When this condition fails, \(X\) is endogenous. Omitted variables, simultaneity, measurement error, and correlated gains can all make \(X\) endogenous.

The simple-regression OLS estimand makes the problem visible:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,Y_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(X_i)} &=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(X_i,\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i\right)} {\operatorname{Var}(X_i)}\\ &=\beta_1+ \underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,u_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(X_i)}} _{\text{endogeneity bias}}. \end{aligned} \]

More data make the OLS estimate more precise around this contaminated quantity; they do not remove the contamination.

The IV idea: use a clean shifter

An instrument \(Z\) changes the endogenous regressor \(X\) but is otherwise unrelated to the outcome disturbance. In the simple linear model, the two core requirements are:

Requirement Population statement Intuition
Relevance \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,X_i)\ne0\) \(Z\) moves \(X\)
Exogeneity and exclusion \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,u_i)=0\) \(Z\) is unrelated to the unobserved causes of \(Y\) and has no direct path to \(Y\)

The covariance between \(Z\) and the outcome is

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,Y_i) &=\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(Z_i,\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i\right)\\ &=\beta_1\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,X_i) +\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,u_i). \end{aligned} \]

If \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,u_i)=0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,X_i)\ne0\), then

\[ \boxed{ \beta_1 =\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,Y_i)} {\operatorname{Cov}(Z_i,X_i)} }. \]

This ratio is the central IV idea:

\[ \boxed{ \text{causal effect of }X\text{ on }Y =\frac{\text{effect of }Z\text{ on }Y} {\text{effect of }Z\text{ on }X} }. \]

The numerator is the reduced form. The denominator is the first stage. Their ratio converts the outcome effect of a one-unit change in \(Z\) into the outcome effect of a one-unit change in \(X\).

Binary instruments and the Wald estimator

When \(Z\in\{0,1\}\), covariance differences simplify to differences in conditional means:

\[ \boxed{ \beta_{Wald} =\frac{E[Y_i\mid Z_i=1]-E[Y_i\mid Z_i=0]} {E[X_i\mid Z_i=1]-E[X_i\mid Z_i=0]} }. \]

This is the Wald estimand. It is especially useful for lotteries, eligibility rules, draft assignment, and randomized encouragement designs.

From identification to estimation

With an intercept, the sample analogue of the simple IV estimand is

\[ \widehat\beta_{IV} =\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Z_i,Y_i)} {\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Z_i,X_i)} =\frac{\sum_i(Z_i-\overline Z)(Y_i-\overline Y)} {\sum_i(Z_i-\overline Z)(X_i-\overline X)}. \]

Equivalently, IV chooses \(\widehat\beta\) so that the sample analogue of the orthogonality condition is zero:

\[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\left(Y_i-X_i'\widehat\beta\right)=0, \]

where the intercept and any exogenous controls are included in \(X_i\) and in the instrument vector.

This moment-condition view scales naturally from one instrument to many.

Multivariate IV

Endogenous regressors, controls, and instruments

Write the structural equation as

\[ Y_i=X_i'\beta+u_i, \]

where \(X_i\) contains \(K\) regressors. Partition it as

\[ X_i= \begin{bmatrix} X_{1i}\\ W_i \end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(X_{1i}\) is endogenous and \(W_i\) contains exogenous controls, including an intercept. Let \(Z_{1i}\) denote the excluded instruments. The full instrument vector is

\[ Z_i= \begin{bmatrix} Z_{1i}\\ W_i \end{bmatrix}. \]

Exogenous controls instrument for themselves. The excluded instruments \(Z_1\) provide the additional variation needed to identify the endogenous variables.

Two conditions matter:

\[ \underbrace{E[Z_i u_i]=0}_{\text{instrument exogeneity}}, \qquad \underbrace{\operatorname{rank}\!\left(E[Z_iX_i']\right)=K}_{\text{relevance / rank}}. \]

The order condition counts instruments: there must be at least as many instruments as regressors, or equivalently at least as many excluded instruments as endogenous regressors. Counting is not enough. The rank condition asks whether the instruments create distinct movements in the endogenous regressors. If every instrument moves two endogenous regressors in exactly the same proportion, the data cannot separate their two causal effects.

Just identification

Stack the observations into \(y\), \(X\), and \(Z\). If the number of instruments equals the number of regressors, the model is just identified and

\[ \boxed{ \widehat\beta_{IV} =(Z'X)^{-1}Z'y }. \]

Substituting \(y=X\beta+u\) gives

\[ \widehat\beta_{IV}-\beta =(Z'X)^{-1}Z'u =\left(\frac{Z'X}{n}\right)^{-1}\frac{Z'u}{n}. \]

This display separates the two identification requirements:

  • \(Z'u/n\overset{p}{\to}0\) means the instruments do not systematically move with the unobserved determinants of \(Y\). This comes from instrument exogeneity.
  • \(Z'X/n\) summarizes all of the first-stage relationships between the instruments and regressors. It must contain enough distinct information to separate the coefficients. With one instrument and one endogenous regressor, this simply means \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z,X)\ne0\). With several endogenous regressors, the instruments must move them in different combinations rather than always moving them together.

When both hold,

\[ \widehat\beta_{IV}\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\beta. \]

Two-stage least squares

Why we need 2SLS

If there are more instruments than regressors, \(Z'X\) is not square and the just-identified formula cannot be inverted. Two-stage least squares (2SLS) combines the instruments into fitted values of the regressors.

Define the projection matrix

\[ P_Z=Z(Z'Z)^{-1}Z'. \]

The projected regressors are

\[ \widetilde X=P_ZX. \]

They are the fitted values from regressing every column of \(X\) on the full instrument set \(Z\). The 2SLS estimator is

\[ \boxed{ \widehat\beta_{2SLS} =(X'P_ZX)^{-1}X'P_Zy =(\widetilde X'\widetilde X)^{-1}\widetilde X'y }. \]

The name describes a useful mental model:

  1. First stage: regress the endogenous variable on all instruments and exogenous controls.
  2. Second stage: use the instrument-predicted variation in the endogenous variable to estimate its effect on the outcome.

How 2SLS, IV, and OLS fit together

2SLS is the general estimator:

  • If the model is just identified, 2SLS reduces to IV.
  • If every regressor serves as its own instrument, so \(Z=X\), IV reduces to OLS.

Moving from OLS to IV therefore changes the source of variation used to estimate \(\beta\): IV keeps only the variation in \(X\) generated by \(Z\).

How IV goes wrong

Invalid or irrelevant instruments

The most direct failures are:

  • Invalid instrument: \(E[Z_iu_i]\ne0\). The instrument carries its own relationship with the outcome disturbance.
  • Irrelevant instrument: \(E[Z_iX_i']\) lacks rank. The instrument does not provide the variation needed to identify the causal coefficient.

Either failure destroys consistency. A nearly irrelevant instrument creates an additional weak-instrument problem even when population relevance is technically nonzero.

Different controls in the two stages

All exogenous controls included in the structural equation must also be included in the instrument set and therefore in the first stage. The matrix estimator enforces this automatically.

Using different control sets changes the projection that defines the instrumented regressor and generally breaks the intended orthogonality conditions. It is no longer the stated 2SLS estimator.

Forbidden regressions

A tempting procedure is to fit a nonlinear first stage - for example, a probit when \(X\) is binary - and then insert its fitted probability into an OLS outcome regression. This is not ordinary 2SLS and is generally inconsistent if the nonlinear first-stage model is misspecified.

For linear IV, a binary endogenous variable does not require a nonlinear first stage. The linear projection remains valid; fitted values need not lie between zero and one because their purpose is projection, not probability forecasting.

Projection and nonlinear transformation do not commute.

Instruments manufactured from functional form

Suppose a researcher wants to estimate the effect of an endogenous variable \(X_1\) but has no external instrument. The regression controls for \(W\), so the researcher proposes using \(W^2\) as an instrument. The argument is that the curved part of the relationship with \(W\) predicts \(X_1\):

\[ W^2 \longrightarrow X_1 \longrightarrow Y. \]

The exclusion restriction now rests entirely on a functional-form choice: \(W\) may affect \(Y\) linearly, but \(W^2\) is assumed to have no direct effect on \(Y\).

For example, let \(W\) be age, \(X_1\) training, and \(Y\) wages. Age squared may predict training, but wage profiles also commonly curve with age. If \(W^2\) belongs in the wage equation, it affects \(Y\) directly and cannot be an excluded instrument.

If the outcome relationship is… Then \(W^2\)…
Truly linear in \(W\) may be excluded from the outcome equation
Curved in \(W\) belongs in the outcome equation and is not a valid excluded instrument

The proposed instrument is therefore valid only if a particular curve is absent from the outcome equation. That is a fragile source of identification because a small functional-form mistake becomes a violation of the exclusion restriction.

Weak instruments

Relevance can be nonzero and still be too small

An instrument is weak when it has little partial correlation with the endogenous regressor after removing the exogenous controls. Population relevance may hold, yet the instrument-predicted component of \(X\) may contain very little signal.

The ratio form explains the instability immediately:

\[ \widehat\beta_{IV} =\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Z,Y)} {\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Z,X)}. \]

To see the amplification directly, suppose the estimated numerator changes only from \(0.20\) to \(0.21\) because of sampling noise:

First-stage denominator \(0.20/\text{denominator}\) \(0.21/\text{denominator}\) Change in IV estimate
\(0.50\) \(0.40\) \(0.42\) \(0.02\)
\(0.02\) \(10.00\) \(10.50\) \(0.50\)

The same \(0.01\) change in the numerator moves the weak-IV estimate by \(0.50\) instead of \(0.02\). Dividing by a small first stage magnifies noise in the reduced form.

Diagnosing first-stage strength

Consider the first stage

\[ X_1=Z_1'\pi_1+W'\pi_2+v. \]

Two related diagnostics are useful:

Diagnostic Construction What it isolates
Partial \(R^2\) Residualize \(X_1\) and every excluded instrument \(Z_1\) with respect to \(W\), then regress the residualized \(X_1\) on the residualized \(Z_1\) Share of residual variation in \(X_1\) explained by excluded instruments
Partial \(F\) statistic Test \(H_0:\pi_1=0\) in the first stage Joint statistical strength of excluded instruments

The ordinary first-stage \(R^2\) is not enough because it can be high solely because the controls \(W\) predict \(X_1\). The partial \(R^2\) asks what the excluded instruments add after those controls have been removed.

For \(L\) excluded instruments, the homoskedastic first-stage statistic has the familiar form

\[ F =\frac{R^2_{\text{partial}}/L} {(1-R^2_{\text{partial}})/(n-\dim(W)-L)}, \]

with degrees of freedom adjusted to the exact specification. The partial \(R^2\) describes population predictive content; the \(F\) statistic also grows with sample size.

More instruments are not automatically better

Instruments added First-stage information Likely consequence
Uninformative None Lower strength per instrument; more finite-sample bias
Highly informative Large Stronger identification
Moderately informative Ambiguous Must evaluate strength and robustness, not instrument count alone

If the number of instruments doubles while their total predictive information does not increase, the first-stage \(F\) statistic can fall sharply. All else equal, a smaller, substantively defensible instrument set is easier to understand and defend.
