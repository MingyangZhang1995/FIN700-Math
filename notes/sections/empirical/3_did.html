Difference-in-Differences

The canonical \(2\times2\) DiD design

The canonical design has two groups and two periods:

Group Pre-treatment: \(t=0\) Post-treatment: \(t=1\)
Treated group: \(G_i=1\) untreated treated
Comparison group: \(G_i=0\) untreated untreated

Define

\[ Post_t=\mathbb{1}\{t=1\}, \qquad D_{it}=G_i\times Post_t. \]

Only the treated group in the post period has \(D_{it}=1\). This is the single cell in which treatment is observed.

Potential outcomes and the target parameter

Let \(Y_{it}(1)\) be unit \(i\)’s outcome at time \(t\) under treatment and \(Y_{it}(0)\) its outcome without treatment. The observed outcome is

\[ Y_{it}=D_{it}Y_{it}(1)+(1-D_{it})Y_{it}(0). \]

The usual target is the post-period average treatment effect on the treated:

\[ \boxed{ ATT(1)=E[Y_{i1}(1)-Y_{i1}(0)\mid G_i=1] }. \]

We observe \(E[Y_{i1}(1)\mid G_i=1]\) but not \(E[Y_{i1}(0)\mid G_i=1]\). DiD is a method for constructing this missing post-treatment counterfactual.

No anticipation

Before treatment begins, treated units must still reveal their untreated potential outcomes:

\[ Y_{i0}(1)=Y_{i0}(0) \qquad\text{for }G_i=1. \]

If units change behavior before formal implementation because treatment is announced or expected, the effective treatment date should be moved earlier. Calling an affected period “pre-treatment” would otherwise contaminate the baseline.

Parallel trends

The identifying assumption is that, without treatment, the two groups would have experienced the same average outcome change:

\[ \boxed{ E[Y_{i1}(0)-Y_{i0}(0)\mid G_i=1] =E[Y_{i1}(0)-Y_{i0}(0)\mid G_i=0] }. \]

Parallel trends allows the groups to have different outcome levels. It restricts their untreated changes.

Under no anticipation and parallel trends, the treated group’s missing counterfactual mean is

\[ \begin{aligned} E[Y_{i1}(0)\mid G_i=1] &=E[Y_{i0}\mid G_i=1]\\ &\quad+ \left( E[Y_{i1}\mid G_i=0]-E[Y_{i0}\mid G_i=0] \right). \end{aligned} \]

Start from the treated group’s pre-treatment level and add the change observed for the comparison group.

Identification by four means

Substitute the identified counterfactual into the definition of \(ATT(1)\):

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} ATT(1) &=\underbrace{ E[Y_{i1}\mid G_i=1]-E[Y_{i0}\mid G_i=1] }_{\text{change for treated group}}\\ &\quad- \underbrace{ \left( E[Y_{i1}\mid G_i=0]-E[Y_{i0}\mid G_i=0] \right) }_{\text{change for comparison group}}. \end{aligned} } \tag{4.1} \]

DiD removes two sources of noncausal difference:

  • The within-group change removes time-invariant group differences.
  • Subtracting the comparison group’s change removes common time shocks.

What remains is attributed to treatment under parallel trends.

A numerical example

Suppose the four sample means are:

Group Pre Post Change
Treated 10 18 \(18-10=8\)
Comparison 8 12 \(12-8=4\)

The comparison group indicates that outcomes would have risen by four even without treatment. The treated group’s counterfactual post-treatment mean is therefore

\[ 10+4=14. \]

The DiD estimate is

\[ \widehat\tau_{DiD} =(18-10)-(12-8)=8-4=4, \]

which is the same as comparing the observed treated post mean with its counterfactual:

\[ 18-14=4. \]

TipTwo equivalent ways to difference

The same estimate can be calculated as a difference in changes,

\[ (18-10)-(12-8), \]

or as a change in cross-group gaps,

\[ (18-12)-(10-8). \]

Both equal four because subtraction can be rearranged.

Estimating the \(2\times2\) design

The sample four-means estimator

Replacing population expectations in Equation 4.1 with sample means gives

\[ \boxed{ \widehat\tau_{DiD} =\left(\overline Y_{1,1}-\overline Y_{1,0}\right) -\left(\overline Y_{0,1}-\overline Y_{0,0}\right), } \]

where \(\overline Y_{g,t}\) is the mean outcome for group \(g\) in period \(t\).

Regression equivalence

The same estimate is the interaction coefficient in the saturated regression

\[ Y_{it} =\alpha+\gamma G_i+\lambda Post_t +\tau(G_i\times Post_t)+u_{it}. \]

The fitted cell means are

Cell Fitted mean
Comparison, pre \(\alpha\)
Treated, pre \(\alpha+\gamma\)
Comparison, post \(\alpha+\lambda\)
Treated, post \(\alpha+\gamma+\lambda+\tau\)

Taking the difference of differences cancels \(\alpha\), \(\gamma\), and \(\lambda\), leaving

\[ \boxed{\tau=DiD.} \]

In the \(2\times2\) design, the four-means calculation and the saturated regression are algebraically identical. The regression is a convenient implementation; the parallel-trends assumption supplies the causal interpretation.

Unit and time fixed effects

With panel data, the same model can be written as

\[ Y_{it}=\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\tau D_{it}+u_{it}. \]

In a genuine \(2\times2\) design, this two-way fixed-effects regression also returns the simple DiD estimate. Unit fixed effects absorb permanent unit differences, and time fixed effects absorb shocks common to all units.

Weights define the target

If units differ in size, weighted and unweighted means answer different questions. For example:

  • An unweighted county analysis targets the average effect across counties.
  • A population-weighted county analysis targets an average effect across people represented by those counties.

When treatment effects are heterogeneous, changing the weights changes the causal parameter. Weights should therefore be chosen to match the population named in the research question, not added mechanically because they improve a regression fit.

Inference

The four cells do not represent four independent observations. Outcomes are usually correlated within units over time and may be correlated within the jurisdiction or institution that assigns treatment.

A useful rule is to align clustering with the level at which treatment varies. If a state adopts a policy and counties are the observations, clustering only by county ignores common state-level shocks. With few assignment clusters, conventional cluster-robust approximations may also be unreliable and require specialized inference.

Evaluating the \(2\times2\) design

Parallel trends is about the untreated counterfactual

The post-treatment part of parallel trends cannot be tested because \(Y_{i1}(0)\) is missing for treated units. Credibility must come from a substantive explanation of why the comparison group captures the treated group’s untreated change.

Ask:

  • Why was the treated group exposed while the comparison group was not?
  • Could that reason also predict different outcome trends?
  • Were other policies or shocks introduced at the same time?
  • Could treatment spill over to the comparison group?
  • Did the composition of either group change?

What pre-treatment trends can tell us

With several pre-treatment periods, we can compare earlier changes across groups. A visible differential pre-trend is evidence against a stable parallel-trends story. Flat pre-treatment event-study estimates are useful, but they do not prove the post-treatment counterfactual assumption:

  • pre-trend tests may have low power;
  • short pre-periods may miss slow-moving differences;
  • anticipation may contaminate periods labeled as untreated; and
  • parallel pre-trends can coexist with a post-period confounder.

Pre-trends are diagnostics and falsification tools, not a certification procedure.

Multiple periods with one treated cohort: \(2\times T\)

Now suppose the same treated group begins treatment in period \(g\), while the comparison group remains untreated throughout. There are still two groups, but there are many periods.

Define the time-specific effect

\[ ATT(t)=E[Y_{it}(1)-Y_{it}(0)\mid G_i=1], \qquad t\ge g. \]

Using the last pre-treatment period \(g-1\) as the baseline, each post-treatment effect is identified by a \(2\times2\) building block:

\[ \boxed{ ATT(t) =E[Y_{it}-Y_{i,g-1}\mid G_i=1] -E[Y_{it}-Y_{i,g-1}\mid G_i=0]. } \tag{4.2} \]

For every post period \(t\), Equation 4.2 uses two groups and two effective periods: the baseline and period \(t\).

Event time and event studies

Define event time as

\[ e=t-g. \]

The treatment period has \(e=0\), the preceding period has \(e=-1\), and the first lag has \(e=1\). A saturated event-study regression for this single treated cohort is

\[ Y_{it} =\alpha_i+\lambda_t +\sum_{e\ne-1}\beta_e \left(G_i\times\mathbb{1}\{t-g=e\}\right) +u_{it}. \]

The omitted period \(e=-1\) is the reference period. With one treated cohort and a never-treated comparison group, each post-treatment coefficient equals the corresponding DiD building block:

\[ \beta_e=ATT(g+e), \qquad e\ge0, \]

under the relevant parallel-trends and no-anticipation assumptions.

For \(e<-1\), the coefficients compare earlier untreated changes with the baseline. They are pre-trend diagnostics, not treatment effects. Joint or simultaneous confidence bands are often more informative than inspecting many pointwise tests separately.

Dynamic effects and aggregation over time

Post-treatment effects may grow, fade, or reverse. Report the event-time path before compressing it into one number. If a summary is needed, define it explicitly:

\[ ATT^{post} =\sum_{t=g}^T \omega_t ATT(t), \qquad \omega_t\ge0, \qquad \sum_{t=g}^T\omega_t=1. \]

Equal weights answer a different question from weights based on the treated population exposed in each period.

Why conventional TWFE can fail

Suppose units adopt the same binary treatment in different periods. Let \(G_i\) denote unit \(i\)’s first treatment date. An absorbing treatment remains on after adoption, so

\[ D_{it}=\mathbb 1\{t\ge G_i\}. \]

This once-treated-always-treated setting keeps the timing transparent. A conventional static two-way fixed-effects regression is

\[ Y_{it}=\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\beta_{TWFE}D_{it}+u_{it}. \]

We can see what this regression compares by working through a toy panel.

A toy panel

Consider two equally sized cohorts observed for three periods. Cohort \(E\) adopts early, in period 1; cohort \(L\) adopts late, in period 2. Each table entry reports the observed outcome followed by treatment status in parentheses.

Cohort First treated \(G\) Period 0 Period 1 Period 2
Early \(E\) 1 \(0\;(D=0)\) \(2\;(D=1)\) \(10\;(D=1)\)
Late \(L\) 2 \(0\;(D=0)\) \(0\;(D=0)\) \(4\;(D=1)\)

Suppose every untreated potential outcome is zero. Parallel trends therefore holds exactly, there is no anticipation, and the treatment effects are

\[ ATT_E(1)=2,\qquad ATT_E(2)=10,\qquad ATT_L(2)=4. \]

We will now calculate what the single TWFE coefficient does with these three positive effects.

Why OLS uses the already-treated cohort

The restriction comes from what each part of TWFE is allowed to explain:

Regression component What it absorbs What it cannot absorb
Unit effect \(\alpha_i\) A permanent outcome level for each unit Any change over time
Time effect \(\lambda_t\) One calendar-time change shared by all units Different time changes for different cohorts
Treatment term \(\beta D_{it}\) One common treatment level shift A treatment effect that grows or shrinks after adoption

Subtract the TWFE equation in period \(t-1\) from the equation in period \(t\). The unit effect disappears:

\[ \Delta Y_{it} =\underbrace{(\lambda_t-\lambda_{t-1})}_{\kappa_t} +\beta_{TWFE}\Delta D_{it} +\Delta u_{it}. \]

Here \(\kappa_t\) is the common time change for interval \(t-1\to t\). The same \(\kappa_t\) must apply to both cohorts. This equation reveals the key mechanical point: any cohort with \(\Delta D=0\) helps determine the common time change, whether that cohort is untreated with \(D=0\) or already treated with \(D=1\).

For the toy panel, the changes are

Interval Early cohort: \(\Delta D_E\) Early cohort: \(\Delta Y_E\) Late cohort: \(\Delta D_L\) Late cohort: \(\Delta Y_L\)
Period \(0\to1\) \(+1\) (switches on) \(+2\) \(0\) (no switch) \(0\)
Period \(1\to2\) \(0\) (already treated) \(+8\) \(+1\) (switches on) \(+4\)

In period \(0\to1\), cohort \(E\) switches on and cohort \(L\) does not. The first-difference equations are

\[ \begin{aligned} E:&\quad 2=\kappa_1+\beta_{0\to1},\\ L:&\quad 0=\kappa_1. \end{aligned} \]

The unchanged late cohort identifies \(\kappa_1=0\), leaving \(\widehat\beta_{0\to1}=2\). Cohort \(L\) is genuinely untreated here, so the comparison is a valid DiD.

In period \(1\to2\), the roles change. Cohort \(E\) is already treated, but its treatment indicator does not change. Cohort \(L\) switches on. The equations are

\[ \begin{aligned} E:&\quad 8=\kappa_2+\beta_{1\to2}(0)=\kappa_2,\\ L:&\quad 4=\kappa_2+\beta_{1\to2}(1). \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\Delta D_E=0\), the model cannot attribute any of cohort \(E\)’s \(8\)-unit increase to the treatment term. It therefore uses that increase to determine the common time change: \(\widehat\kappa_2=8\). The same time change is then applied to cohort \(L\), so its treatment coefficient must explain only the difference between its observed increase and \(8\):

\[ \widehat\beta_{1\to2}=4-\widehat\kappa_2=4-8=-4. \]

This is why TWFE uses the already-treated cohort as a control. With no never-treated group in period 2, cohort \(E\) is the only observation with no change in \(D\). The regression has no indicator saying “already treated and still accumulating effects”; it sees only \(\Delta D_E=0\) and uses \(E\) to identify the common time change.

The second comparison is not a valid untreated counterfactual. Its outcome change contains continued growth in the early cohort’s treatment effect:

\[ \widehat\beta_{1\to2} =ATT_L(2)-\{ATT_E(2)-ATT_E(1)\} =4-(10-2) =-4. \]

The model contains only one treatment coefficient. Because the two cohorts have equal sizes and the two intervals contain the same amount of treatment-status variation, OLS gives the two interval contrasts equal weight. In this simplest Goodman–Bacon decomposition,

\[ \widehat\beta_{TWFE} =\frac12\widehat\beta_{0\to1} +\frac12\widehat\beta_{1\to2} =\frac12(2)+\frac12(-4) =-1. \]

We can now see the failure: the regression reports \(-1\) even though the three treatment effects in the data are \(2\), \(10\), and \(4\). Fixed effects remove cohort levels and common time shocks, but they do not make the already-treated early cohort a valid untreated comparison.

Modern DiD

We now compare two leading implementations of this strategy: the group-time ATT estimators of Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) and the interaction-weighted event-study estimator of Sun and Abraham (2021).

Callaway–Sant’Anna: group-time DiD contrasts

Callaway and Sant’Anna estimate a separate effect for cohort \(g\) in calendar period \(t\):

\[ ATT(g,t) =E[Y_{it}(g)-Y_{it}(\infty)\mid G_i=g]. \]

Here \(G_i=g\) means that unit \(i\) is first treated in period \(g\), while \(G_i=\infty\) denotes a never-treated unit.

What the input data look like

The estimator is naturally fed long panel data: one row for each unit and period, with an outcome \(Y_{it}\), a unit identifier, calendar time \(t\), and first-treatment date \(G_i\). Treatment status can be constructed as \(D_{it}=\mathbb 1\{t\ge G_i\}\).

For compactness, the table below displays cohort means. Unit-level data repeat this structure for every unit.

Cohort \(G_i\) \(t\) \(Y_{it}\) \(D_{it}\) Event time \(e=t-G_i\)
Early \(E\) 2 0 20 0 \(-2\)
Early \(E\) 2 1 21 0 \(-1\)
Early \(E\) 2 2 25 1 \(0\)
Early \(E\) 2 3 29 1 \(1\)
Late \(L\) 3 0 30 0 \(-3\)
Late \(L\) 3 1 31 0 \(-2\)
Late \(L\) 3 2 32 0 \(-1\)
Late \(L\) 3 3 37 1 \(0\)
Never \(N\) \(\infty\) 0 10 0 –
Never \(N\) \(\infty\) 1 11 0 –
Never \(N\) \(\infty\) 2 12 0 –
Never \(N\) \(\infty\) 3 13 0 –

The untreated outcome grows by one per period in every cohort. The table therefore builds parallel untreated trends directly into the example. The treatment effects are \(3\) and \(6\) for the early cohort and \(4\) for the late cohort.

The same data can be read as a treatment-timing map:

Cohort Period 0 Period 1 Period 2 Period 3
Early \(E\) Untreated Untreated Treated Treated
Late \(L\) Untreated Untreated Untreated Treated
Never \(N\) Untreated Untreated Untreated Untreated

What the estimator does

Using never-treated cohort \(N\), each \(ATT(g,t)\) is an ordinary two-group, two-period DiD. The baseline is the cohort’s last untreated period \(g-1\):

\[ ATT(g,t) =E[Y_{it}-Y_{i,g-1}\mid G_i=g] -E[Y_{it}-Y_{i,g-1}\mid G_i=\infty]. \]

For the early cohort in its treatment period,

\[ \widehat{ATT}(2,2) =(25-21)-(12-11) =3. \]

For that cohort one period after treatment,

\[ \widehat{ATT}(2,3) =(29-21)-(13-11) =6. \]

For the late cohort in its treatment period,

\[ \widehat{ATT}(3,3) =(37-32)-(13-12) =4. \]

The estimator repeats this calculation cell by cell. The following map makes the comparison sets explicit.

Effect cell Change for treated cohort Eligible untreated controls Who is excluded from the control group?
\(ATT(2,2)\) \(E:1\to2\) \(N\), or \(L\) and \(N\) if using not-yet-treated controls No earlier-treated cohort is eligible
\(ATT(2,3)\) \(E:1\to3\) \(N\) \(L\) is treated by period 3
\(ATT(3,3)\) \(L:2\to3\) \(N\) \(E\) is already treated

With not-yet-treated controls, eligibility is imposed mechanically by \(G_i>t\). Thus \(E\), with \(G_i=2\), cannot be a control for \(ATT(3,3)\) because \(2>3\) is false. With never-treated controls, only \(G_i=\infty\) is retained. This is exactly how Callaway–Sant’Anna prevents already-treated outcomes from serving as untreated counterfactuals.

To form an event-time estimate, the method aggregates cells with the same \(e=t-g\). If the treated cohorts are equally sized,

\[ \widehat\theta_{CS}(0) =\frac12\widehat{ATT}(2,2) +\frac12\widehat{ATT}(3,3) =\frac12(3)+\frac12(4) =3.5, \]

while only the early cohort is observed at \(e=1\), so

\[ \widehat\theta_{CS}(1)=\widehat{ATT}(2,3)=6. \]

Sun–Abraham: the interaction-weighted event study

Sun and Abraham use the same long panel structure, but encode treatment with cohort-by-event-time interaction columns. Their key move is to estimate a saturated regression before averaging across cohorts.

What “saturated” means

A conventional event study creates one event-time-zero indicator that equals one for every cohort in its first treated period. In the example, it puts \((E,t=2)\) and \((L,t=3)\) in the same column and forces them to share one coefficient.

A dummy variable is simply a column of zeros and ones. Its coefficient lets the fitted outcome be higher or lower for the rows in which the dummy equals one. For example, a dummy labeled \(E\times e{=}1\) equals one only for observations from cohort \(E\) one period after treatment and equals zero everywhere else.

A saturated event study creates one such dummy for every supported cohort-event-time pair:

\[ \mathbb 1\{G_i=g\}\mathbb 1\{t-g=e\}. \]

Using \(e=-1\) as the omitted reference period, the regression is

\[ Y_{it} =\alpha_i+\lambda_t +\sum_g\sum_{e\ne-1} \delta_{g,e} \mathbb 1\{G_i=g\}\mathbb 1\{t-g=e\} +u_{it}. \]

For the numerical panel above, the post-treatment portion of the design matrix is

Cohort and time \(Y_{it}\) \(E\times e{=}0\) \(E\times e{=}1\) \(L\times e{=}0\)
\(E,t=0\) 20 0 0 0
\(E,t=1\) (reference) 21 0 0 0
\(E,t=2\) 25 1 0 0
\(E,t=3\) 29 0 1 0
\(L,t=0\) 30 0 0 0
\(L,t=1\) 31 0 0 0
\(L,t=2\) (reference) 32 0 0 0
\(L,t=3\) 37 0 0 1
Every \(N,t\) row 10, 11, 12, 13 0 0 0

Unit fixed effects absorb permanent level differences, and time fixed effects absorb the one-unit common trend. Never-treated cohort \(N\) is omitted from the interaction columns and anchors the untreated time effects.

The three interaction coefficients recover three separate DiDs:

\[ \widehat\delta_{E,0} =(25-21)-(12-11) =3, \]

\[ \widehat\delta_{E,1} =(29-21)-(13-11) =6, \]

and

\[ \widehat\delta_{L,0} =(37-32)-(13-12) =4. \]

Why separate dummies prevent the already-treated comparison

Focus on period 3. Cohort \(L\) is newly treated, cohort \(E\) is already treated, and cohort \(N\) remains untreated.

Begin with the outcome the model predicts in the absence of a cohort-event-time effect. The cohort fixed effect supplies each cohort’s period-0 level: \(10\) for \(N\), \(20\) for \(E\), and \(30\) for \(L\). The time fixed effect adds the common trend: \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\) in periods 0 through 3. The remaining differences are handled by the separate dummy columns.

Cohort-time cell Cohort level plus common time effect Dummy that equals one Amount added by its coefficient Fitted outcome
\(N,t=3\) \(10+3=13\) None \(0\) \(13\)
\(E,t=2\) \(20+2=22\) \(E\times e{=}0\) \(\delta_{E,0}=3\) \(25\)
\(E,t=3\) \(20+3=23\) \(E\times e{=}1\) \(\delta_{E,1}=6\) \(29\)
\(L,t=3\) \(30+3=33\) \(L\times e{=}0\) \(\delta_{L,0}=4\) \(37\)

“Including the \(E\times e{=}1\) dummy” therefore has a literal meaning: the model is allowed to add a number only to cohort \(E\)’s period-3 observations. The fitted equation for that cell is

\[ 29 =\underbrace{20}_{\text{cohort level}} +\underbrace{3}_{\text{common time effect}} +\underbrace{6}_{E\times e=1\text{ coefficient}}. \]

Likewise, the newly treated late cohort has its own equation:

\[ 37 =\underbrace{30}_{\text{cohort level}} +\underbrace{3}_{\text{common time effect}} +\underbrace{4}_{L\times e=0\text{ coefficient}}. \]

Cohort \(N\) has zeros in every treatment-dummy column. Its period-3 outcome, \(13=10+3\), reveals the untreated common time effect. Cohort \(E\)’s extra \(6\) and cohort \(L\)’s extra \(4\) are then recorded in different columns and are free to differ.

To see why the separate dummy matters, imagine omitting \(E\times e{=}1\). The model would have no term specifically for the extra \(6\) observed for \(E\) in period 3. It would have to leave that difference unexplained or push part of it into the period-3 time effect. Because the same time effect is also subtracted from cohort \(L\), the early cohort’s growing treatment effect would contaminate \(\delta_{L,0}\).

With \(E\times e{=}1\) included, the extra \(6\) has its own place in the regression. It does not need to alter the common time effect used to construct \(L\)’s untreated counterfactual. The already-treated observations from \(E\) remain in the dataset, but they are no longer forced to behave like untreated observations: their cohort-specific treatment effect is explicitly represented by their own dummy.

The interaction-weighted step then averages cohort-specific coefficients at the same event time. With equally sized cohorts,

\[ \widehat\theta_{SA}(0) =\frac12\widehat\delta_{E,0} +\frac12\widehat\delta_{L,0} =3.5, \]

and

\[ \widehat\theta_{SA}(1) =\widehat\delta_{E,1} =6. \]

The two estimators coincide in this example because they use the same never-treated comparison cohort, the same cohort-event-time cells, and the same cohort weights.
